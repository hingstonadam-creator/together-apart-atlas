Exegesis

Graduate project / ARCH 6004

Together, Apart. — Proximity Without Dependence

This exegesis follows the development of a housing proposal at Southwark Grounds. It considers how related households can live close together while retaining the independence of their own homes, and how entrances, outdoor spaces and shared routes can allow residents to choose when they connect.

It begins with the project position, reviews the research that informed it, and then follows the design approach, development, proposal and reflection. Each chapter connects the written argument to drawings, models and observations from the project.

At Southwark Grounds, the project asks how residents can live near family while retaining control over everyday contact. The Position chapter establishes the project’s scope and the shift from The Civic Skin to domestic thresholds.

Chapter 01

Position

Project overview

How close would you choose to live to family if you could still close your own front door?

Together, Apart investigates how apartments can support downsizing and multigenerational living while giving residents choices about privacy, independence and shared life. Located at Southwark Grounds, the proposal accommodates families in homes of their own, related households in connected dwellings and several generations within one home. These arrangements explore how housing can respond to different routines and relationships, including the desire to live close to family while retaining control over everyday contact.

The proposal develops through horizontal, vertical and joint arrangements. Homes beside or above one another retain independent entrances alongside a direct family connection. The joint XL dwelling brings several generations under one roof, with an entrance arrangement that allows a degree of separation within the home. Together, these explore how the organisation of rooms, doors and outdoor spaces can accommodate different degrees of sharing and independence.

An occupiable building envelope extends this investigation beyond the apartment interior. Entrance alcoves, galleries, deep balconies and terraces provide space for arrival, gardening, outdoor activities and personal adaptation. Drawing on qualities associated with yards, verandas, gates and individual front doors, I explore how residents can establish a domestic territory within an apartment building. DEFINE, EXTEND and MEDIATE organise this approach through the boundaries of the home, the extension of domestic life outdoors and the relationships between households.

The project developed from The Civic Skin, an earlier investigation into terracotta fabrication and the occupation of a façade research centre’s edge. Moving into housing brought that interest into everyday domestic life. At Southwark Grounds, it also extends to the building’s relationship with the existing promenade, where the ground floor provides space for public life. The enquiry moves between the individual home, connections across households and the building’s contribution to its surrounding community.

This exegesis brings together my research, Learning Journal and design work to explain how the project developed. Plans and sections explore how the different household arrangements could work in everyday use, including opportunities to share space and maintain privacy. Together, these studies record the decisions that shaped the proposal and the questions that remain open.

Southwark Grounds and the scale of the enquiry

The site at Southwark Grounds sits along an existing promenade within a new development. The project looks at how the ground-floor spaces, including the courtyard and brick entry pavilion, can support public life and contribute to the surrounding community. The scale of enquiry therefore runs from the individual dwelling to shared spaces between households and out to public spaces at ground level. Each scale asks how space can be made available to others while residents maintain privacy and control over their homes.

Reframing the project

Moving from The Civic Skin to housing changed how I understood the occupiable envelope. The façade research centre explored how a building edge could offer shade, a place to pause and opportunities for public encounter. In housing, these spaces became part of everyday domestic life, accommodating arrival, time outdoors, contact with family and opportunities to retreat.

Residents’ choice became central to this shift. Living close to family can make everyday contact and support easier, while each household still needs space for its own routines. The proposal explores how residents can choose when to spend time together and when to withdraw, through the relationship between entrances, living spaces and outdoor areas.

The vertical arrangement connects homes on different levels, while the horizontal arrangement places related households beside one another. Both allow families to live in their own homes with a direct connection. The joint arrangement accommodates several generations within one dwelling, bringing greater emphasis to the relationship between shared living areas and private retreat. Together, these arrangements explore different degrees of independence and sharing while living close to family.

Chapter 02

Research

Research method and evidence

My research brings together 101 entries drawn from previous projects, a site visit, conversations with practitioners, surveys, journal articles, videos, podcasts and books. These sources helped me explore how people occupy space through physical, psychological and philosophical perspectives, with a particular interest in domestic life in Australia.

The research began with The Civic Skin and the idea of an occupiable building envelope. It developed into a broader investigation of how apartments could better support everyday life, then focused on downsizing and multigenerational living. Throughout this process, I considered how spaces accommodate different routines, expectations of privacy and choices about spending time with others.

The database records this development and brings different sources into conversation. Reading across built projects, research and accounts of everyday experience helped me develop the project’s position and identify questions to explore through design.

Using precedents as questions for the design

My precedent research focused on single-family and multifamily dwellings, looking closely at their spatial arrangements and how privacy is negotiated within the home. I considered how bedrooms, living areas, entrances and outdoor spaces relate to one another, and how these relationships allow people to share a home while maintaining their own routines.

These studies helped me frame questions to explore within an apartment setting. How can related households live close together and retain independence? How can several generations share a dwelling with space for privacy? I explored these questions through the vertical, horizontal and joint arrangements, considering how connections between homes and spaces within them could support different ways of living.

This also informed my thinking about the occupiable envelope. Balconies, terraces and entrance spaces extend the places available for everyday activities, creating opportunities to spend time together or find space away from shared living areas.

Chapter 03

Design approach

DEFINE, EXTEND and MEDIATE organise my approach to domestic independence, outdoor occupation and the relationships between households. Together, they explore how residents can make a home of their own while choosing how they connect with others.

DEFINE

DEFINE considers the boundaries of each dwelling and how these establish domestic independence. An entrance alcove or recessed doorway marks the transition from outside to inside, giving residents a space associated with their own home. My research into traditional Japanese architecture informed this interest in how an entrance can establish a boundary through a sequence of spaces.

This also applies to the boundary between neighbouring homes. The proposal considers how related households can remain distinct, even where they share an alcove or have a direct connection. Defining these territories gives each household control over access to its home.

EXTEND

EXTEND considers how outdoor space can become part of the domestic territory of an apartment. The proposal uses balconies approximately 3.5 metres deep to accommodate a wider range of everyday activities, including gardening, working with tools and spending time outdoors. This additional space gives residents more ways to use an apartment while maintaining privacy and independence.

The depth also creates opportunities for residents to adapt the space as their needs change. I am interested in how part of this outdoor territory might eventually be enclosed to provide additional internal space, and how the building envelope could accommodate that change.

MEDIATE

MEDIATE considers collective relationships and the shared routes between entrances. In the horizontal arrangement, neighbouring households can share an entrance alcove while retaining separate front doors. In the joint XL dwelling, a shared entrance provides direct access to one bedroom, allowing a degree of separation from the main family living area.

In the vertical arrangement, an additional connection allows family to visit through a back door while each household retains its own independent arrival. This draws on the familiarity of family and close friends coming around through the back door. These different arrangements explore how entrances and connections can support everyday contact while giving residents choices about privacy and independence.

Chapter 04

Development

Material exploration and The Civic Skin

I began the semester with an interest in 3D printing, extruded terracotta and how façade elements are made. I wanted to understand how their shape could influence material use and environmental performance. Research into terracotta profiles introduced possibilities for using less material and affecting how heat is reflected into the surrounding environment. This led me to consider how the façade could contribute to more comfortable public spaces, including shaded thresholds and places to pause.

The Civic Skin, a façade research centre, gave these interests a programme. As the proposal developed, however, I felt that its architectural organisation and purpose needed a stronger argument. Feedback reinforced this concern and prompted me to reconsider where the material and fabrication research could lead.

From The Civic Skin to domestic cladding

Through tutor discussions, I began exploring how the technology investigated in The Civic Skin could be applied to domestic architecture. My attention moved towards cladding and prefabricated façade elements, considering how these could improve housing quality and speed up the production of new homes.

This direction connected my interest in fabrication with concerns about the housing crisis and productivity in the construction industry. It also raised questions about what these elements could offer the people living behind them. I began considering how the façade could contribute to the use of a home, alongside its construction and environmental performance.

From cladding to apartment living

As I looked more closely at housing, my attention shifted towards domestic life in apartments. I became interested in perceived privacy and the transitions between public, shared and private space. I wanted to explore how apartments could offer qualities associated with detached and semi-detached homes, including yards, fences, gates, verandas and individual front doors.

These observations informed six principles—arrival, your own front door, inside–outside, privacy gradient, neighbourhood and individual identity. They guided an approach that provided public space, opportunities for personalisation, clear thresholds and an occupiable façade. The envelope became part of the apartment’s domestic territory, offering space for everyday activities outdoors while allowing residents to establish boundaries around their homes.

From apartments to multigenerational living

Feedback in October challenged the broad audience of the apartment proposal. I had been considering what might improve housing for the general population, but this had led to a direction that was too generic. The critique encouraged me to focus on more specific circumstances. I began exploring downsizing and multigenerational living, considering how proximity to family could work alongside different needs for privacy, independence and shared space.

This changed how I approached the dwelling plans and their aggregation. I moved from considering an apartment individually to exploring how two homes could work together and how these relationships could shape the building. Horizontally connected homes, vertically connected homes and a joint multigenerational dwelling became three arrangements through which to develop the proposal. Each gave the entrances, outdoor spaces and connections between homes a more specific role in supporting everyday family life.

Chapter 05

Proposal

The inhabited threshold

The proposal brings together independent family homes, connected dwellings and a larger shared home for several generations. Entrances, galleries, balconies and terraces form an occupiable envelope around these homes, giving residents places to spend time outdoors and choices about how they connect with family and neighbours.

The proposed housing arrangement

The dwelling system uses S, M, L and XL types to accommodate different household arrangements. In the horizontal arrangement, S sits beside M, allowing related households to occupy separate homes with a direct connection. In the vertical arrangement, S sits above L, with an additional family connection between levels. Each home retains its own front door, allowing residents to arrive independently and maintain their own routines.

The XL dwelling accommodates several generations within one home. A shared entrance gives direct access to one bedroom, providing a degree of separation from the main family living area. This arrangement explores how shared domestic life can accommodate different needs for privacy and independence under the same roof.

External galleries connect the homes to the wider building. Entrance alcoves establish a space around each front door, while planting and screening help define the relationship between shared routes and domestic territory. Deep balconies and terraces extend the activities available within the apartments, providing room for gardening, working with tools and spending time outdoors.

At ground level, the proposal connects with the existing promenade and provides space for public life. The courtyard and brick entry pavilion form part of this relationship between the surrounding area and the homes above. The sequence from public ground-floor space to shared galleries and individual entrances allows the building to accommodate different degrees of access and occupation.

Independent homes, shared resources

The original Athanor investigation explored how a data centre could contribute to the city around it. At Southwark Grounds, this idea continues through a data centre beneath the development, proposed as a source of recovered heat for the homes above and shared ground-floor facilities.

The proposal investigates how heat generated by computing equipment could be recovered and distributed through a central energy plant to support residential heating and domestic hot water. Each household would retain control over its internal environment while drawing on shared infrastructure.

Preliminary calculations for a 150 kW data centre suggest that the heat potentially available could exceed the development’s annual heating requirements. The question is also when that heat is available and where it can be used. Winter demand could provide an opportunity for heat recovery, while lower summer demand would require other uses, storage or heat rejection. Future connections could extend the network to neighbouring buildings as the precinct develops.

This carries the position of Together, Apart beyond the arrangement of dwellings. Each household retains its independence, while the infrastructure supporting it becomes a shared resource. The study explores how heat produced on site could contribute to the building and, potentially, the wider neighbourhood.

The proposed capacities and recovery estimates are preliminary design calculations requiring further technical verification.

Material decisions as part of the spatial argument

The material palette combines Paperbark cladding, brick at the lower levels, spotted gum and coloured concrete and steel elements. Pale eucalyptus green identifies domestic and private spaces, including the circulation associated with them. A reddish colour marks public circulation and thresholds, carrying the colour of the ground-level brick into concrete and steel elsewhere in the building.

These colours help distinguish the territories encountered when moving through the proposal, from public spaces at ground level to the more private spaces around the homes. The palette works alongside entrances, planting and screening to make these transitions legible.

Chapter 06

Reflection

Everyday occupation and domestic independence

One of the main things I learned through this project was to think more carefully about how spaces are arranged and what people encounter as they move through a home. I became more aware of how an entrance affects privacy, including which rooms someone passes before reaching the main living area. This brought greater attention to the gradient between public, shared and private spaces. It also changed how I understood the threshold. The entrance became part of a wider sequence that shapes how residents arrive, interact with others and withdraw into their own space.

The joint XL dwelling gave me a specific way to explore this. I considered how an older resident, an au pair or another household member might use the same front door while following a different routine from the rest of the family. Providing direct access from the entrance to a bedroom with an ensuite allows that person to come and go without moving through the main family living area. They can still participate in shared life, with some control over when that happens. I also began to see how this arrangement could accommodate a teenager later, allowing the same space to support changing household circumstances.

Individual identity and the occupiable envelope

Another important development was thinking about individual identity within apartments. Earlier in the project, I had been interested in fabrication and the façade as a building element. Through the housing investigation, I became more interested in what residents could do with the space around their homes. I began considering whether the repetition associated with apartment buildings could accommodate more personal expression, and how additional outdoor space might give residents opportunities to make their home recognisably their own.

The deep balconies became central to this thinking. Their size allows for activities such as gardening, working with tools and spending time outdoors, while also offering possibilities for residents to adapt the space. I imagined someone adding a veranda or another structure to suit how they wanted to live. My intention was to arrange these spaces so that personal additions could be accommodated with consideration for neighbouring homes, their outlook and their outdoor territory. This helped me understand the occupiable envelope as something that could develop through use, with residents contributing to the character of the building over time.

Developing the whole building

The project also made me more aware of where I place my attention when designing. I am particularly interested in the smaller scale of everyday occupation, including how someone enters a home, uses an outdoor space or visits family nearby. Aggregation became a way to develop the building through relationships between dwellings. Working through the vertical, horizontal and joint arrangements gave me a clearer understanding of how independence and connection could be organised spatially. These studies express the aspects of housing that matter most to me.

However, the overall form received uneven attention. I focused heavily on the western side because it had the strongest relationship with public activity and the surrounding site. That gave this side a clear role, while the eastern side remained less developed. Looking back, I would have explored the building more fully from both sides and considered how the household arrangements could inform the whole development. Some of the ideas I wanted to pursue became clearer towards the end of the semester, leaving limited time to work them back into the massing.

Timing resolution and what I would carry forward

The changes in direction were important to the development of the project. Moving towards downsizing and multigenerational living gave the housing proposal a more specific purpose and helped me make decisions about entrances, connections and outdoor spaces. At the same time, making this pivot late in the semester created time constraints that affected how far I could refine those decisions. I could explore the main ideas within particular parts of the proposal, but had less opportunity to develop them consistently across the building.

If I began again, I would give more detailed attention to the internal arrangements and spatial requirements of the dwellings before developing the massing. I had an approximate understanding of the space each type required, and I established rules about which homes would sit beside or above one another. Resolving those homes more thoroughly at that stage could have given the aggregation a stronger basis. I would carry the relationships between rooms, entrances and outdoor areas into the larger arrangement earlier, then move between those scales as the design developed.

The vertical, horizontal and joint arrangements remain the clearest expression of what I wanted to achieve. They explore how families can live close together with different degrees of sharing and independence. I think these ideas are achieved within smaller sections of the development, while their influence across the whole proposal is less resolved. Moving in this direction earlier would have allowed more time to develop that consistency. The project has clarified my interest in the human relationships within housing and shown me where those concerns need to be carried further into the organisation and form of a building.
