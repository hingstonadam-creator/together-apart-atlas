Chapter 01 / Position

Independent homes, connected lives

How can multigenerational housing support everyday family connection while preserving each household’s independence?

Project overview

Together, Apart investigates how multigenerational housing can support everyday connection while preserving the independence of each household. Located at Southwark Grounds, the proposal brings self-sufficient homes into relationships of adjacency, shared access and outdoor occupation. Its focus is the space between the individual dwelling and the collective building: the front door, entrance alcove, gallery and terrace. These spaces are treated as places where contact can be negotiated rather than assumed.

The project develops from an earlier investigation, The Civic Skin, into occupied environmental edges. The programme has changed from a façade research centre to housing, shifting the emphasis from public encounter with a technical building to domestic agency. Research is organised around independent homes, related-household clusters and the wider community. Precedent analysis and the proposed comparison of access arrangements inform a design strategy expressed through DEFINE, EXTEND and MEDIATE. [R111–R116]

The proposal remains a design investigation. Its drawings can demonstrate spatial arrangements and alternative occupancy conditions; they cannot establish that particular social or care outcomes will follow. The exegesis therefore distinguishes documented decisions, retrospective interpretation and tests still to be completed. It brings the research record and Learning Journal into one account of how the project’s argument has developed.

Southwark Grounds and the scale of the enquiry

Southwark Grounds provides the setting for an investigation into the relationship between the individual home and shared urban life. The housing direction places the everyday arrival sequence at the centre of that enquiry. The public approach, building entrance, shared route and dwelling threshold need to form a legible sequence, while maintaining a distinction between access to the building and access to a resident’s private life.

The studio situates Southwark and Karrawirra Parri on Kaurna Country and requires attention to historical, cultural, social and environmental context. This demands more than a generic site description. The completed account needs to identify how verified knowledge of Country and the site changes a particular design decision. No consultation or cultural authority is claimed in this draft.

Reframing the project: from civic edge to domestic threshold

The move from The Civic Skin to multigenerational housing changed both the programme and the people whose everyday lives the project addresses. The earlier proposal investigated the edge of a technical building as a place of public encounter. The housing proposal brings that interest closer to the front door: the threshold now has to negotiate arrival, retreat, family contact and the ordinary routines of living beside others. I interpret this as continuity in the question of how an edge can be occupied, alongside a substantial revision of its social purpose. [R111–R112]

This distinction matters because the housing scheme cannot be justified simply by carrying forward the language of the earlier project. A civic threshold may invite public access; a domestic threshold must also allow a resident to refuse it. The design position therefore needs two commitments at once: opportunities for connection and a credible capacity for independence. Separate entrances, self-sufficient homes and private outdoor space become criteria against which shared spaces must be judged. [R115]

The intended audience is households seeking proximity across generations without assuming that all daily life should be shared. This is a design position, not a demonstrated account of demand. I need to distinguish the preferences embedded in my brief from evidence gathered from residents. The project can propose ways of organising this relationship, but claims about whom it benefits must remain qualified until they are supported by direct research.

Draft basis: R111, R112, R115; retrospective interpretation of the documented pivot.

Chapter 02 / Research

From evidence to spatial choices

The research is used to identify spatial mechanisms and the limits of what those mechanisms can demonstrate.

Research method and evidence

The research record contains 118 active entries assembled from the project’s Notion database. It includes earlier façade and civic-edge enquiries alongside later housing research. These entries form an evidence trail, but they are not all equivalent: published projects, theoretical positions, reported feedback and design intentions have different roles. The exegesis uses the later housing records to clarify the current proposition while retaining the earlier material to make the pivot traceable.

The method is to connect a research observation to a design implication and then to a drawing or model capable of testing it. The selected housing precedents are grouped by the scale of the relationship they address. A small dwelling may inform internal flexibility, a paired house may inform optional connection, and a collective housing project may inform shared provision. Transferring a mechanism requires checking what changes when its scale, residents or management context changes. [R101–R110]

The current synthesis is based on recorded database properties. A complete bibliography and the underlying publications are still needed to substantiate the final academic account. Interview statements must be attributed to the relevant person, date and context; a note from the earlier programme cannot automatically be treated as evidence of housing residents’ needs. Research identifiers in this manuscript refer to the appendix rather than replacing formal citations.

Using precedents as questions for the design

The housing precedents are most useful when they help me specify a relationship to test. The research notes distinguish adaptable arrangements within a home, connections between separate homes, and shared provision at the scale of a building or neighbourhood. These are different propositions. Treating all of them as examples of multigenerational living would obscure the choices my own scheme needs to make. [R101–R110]

At the dwelling scale, the question is whether two daily routines can coexist without one household depending on another for basic access or services. At the cluster scale, the question is how connection can remain optional. At the building scale, it is whether shared facilities offer a practical benefit and a clear responsibility for their operation. This reading gives the precedent study a purpose beyond collecting formal references: each example should lead to a specific plan, section or occupancy comparison in my project. [R101–R109]

There are limits to what I can transfer. A cooperative project carries organisational arrangements that a drawing alone cannot reproduce. A completed building and an unbuilt proposal also provide different kinds of evidence. I should not treat either an attractive shared space or a published design intention as proof of social outcomes. The next stage of the research is to attach original sources and comparable drawings to these interpretations, identifying precisely which spatial mechanism I have adopted and which assumptions I have left behind. [R105–R110]

Draft basis: R101–R110; interpretations of database summaries, not a fresh review of original publications.

Charles House [R101]

Use XL as a home with two credible daily routines, rather than a large apartment with an extra bedroom.

Design application — Draw XL in three states: young family; grandparent co-residence; adult-child independence.

Qualification — Adaptable rooms alone do not establish two fully autonomous dwellings. Test services, entry and acoustic separation in each state.

Mahogany House [R102]

A shared threshold can connect two homes while their living rooms remain distinct.

Design application — Compare direct-facing and offset living-room windows around an S–M shared front yard.

Qualification — Courtyard-facing windows can still expose private life. Separation of mass does not prove visual or acoustic privacy.

3 Generation House [R103]

This is the closest match to your S-above-L rule: vertical proximity needs an independent accessible route.

Design application — Draw S-above-L with the lift route, optional family link and a closed-link state.

Qualification — A private stair alone would not offer equivalent access. Adaptation must preserve independent entrances and services.

Veil + Co. House [R104]

Translate the covered street into a sheltered gallery with distinct front doors and places to pause.

Design application — Draw a gallery-to-front-yard section in open and screened states, including rain cover and a clear bypass.

Qualification — Do not copy the garage into your no-carparking brief. Screens require orientation-specific testing; they do not establish acoustic separation.

La Borda [R105]

Make shared laundry a useful, accessible destination; do not require a resident to socialise to perform a necessary task.

Design application — Locate laundry on an everyday route with a quiet bypass, seating and a management note.

Qualification — Cooperative governance is part of the model. Shared provision cannot be credited with cost or care benefits without operational evidence.

Marmalade Lane [R106]

Give the cluster a recognisable shared address, but retain each home's own door and outdoor retreat.

Design application — Test the same four-home cluster with related families and unrelated households.

Qualification — A self-selecting cohousing group is not equivalent to all future residents. Translate the spatial options without assuming universal participation.

Giesserei [R107]

A large building can contain several small neighbourhoods. Group S/M/L around a legible landing rather than one anonymous corridor.

Design application — Compare a cluster landing and long gallery for homes served, route length and sightlines.

Qualification — Continuous exterior layers can also create overlooking. Room variety needs allocation rules and management.

Generationernes Hus [R108]

Distinguish informal neighbourly help from professional care. Your family cluster can support proximity without becoming an aged-care facility.

Design application — Map voluntary family contact separately from deliveries, visitors and any future care visit.

Qualification — Co-location does not prove interaction. Operational staffing cannot be replaced by architecture or unpaid family labour.

Hunziker Areal / mehr als wohnen [R109]

Offer several degrees of connection: independent home, family cluster, shared building and public street.

Design application — Assign each shared space a user group, catchment and responsible manager; remove redundant facilities.

Qualification — Do not transfer the resources of a 13-building cooperative directly to one six-storey project.

Strandeiland M3 [R110]

Your spatial cluster also needs an occupancy strategy if adjacent S and M homes are intended for related households.

Design application — Test allocation, turnover and unrelated occupancy without changing the architectural shell.

Qualification — This is design-intent evidence, not a completed or post-occupancy precedent. The 344 figure covers the collaborative development, not BETA's block alone.

Chapter 03 / Design approach

Define / Extend / Mediate

Define domestic independence; extend domestic territory; mediate collective relationships.

Independence as a test of connection

DEFINE, EXTEND and MEDIATE provide a way to explain the proposal at the scale of everyday occupation. DEFINE establishes the home as an independent territory. EXTEND considers the outdoor space associated with that home. MEDIATE addresses the relationship between these territories and shared movement. Their value depends on whether the same piece of plan can demonstrate all three without one function undermining another.

The S–M adjacency and S-above-L rule establishes proximity between related households, but adjacency alone does not establish a workable connection. The route between homes, the option to close a link, and the ability to arrive independently need to be drawn. A vertical relationship is particularly important to test: a private stair may offer convenient family contact for some residents while excluding others. I therefore need to assess the independent arrival route separately from any optional internal connection. [R116]

The threshold is also a potential point of conflict. A seat, planter or screen may enrich arrival, but it occupies space and changes visibility. My proposed test is to draw the door swing, clear movement route, pause space and sightlines together, then compare open and screened conditions. This would allow the claimed generosity of the threshold to be assessed alongside privacy and access. At present, these are criteria for evaluation rather than completed findings. [R112, R115]

Draft basis: R112, R115, R116; DEFINE / EXTEND / MEDIATE comes from the project instructions of 27 October and 2 November 2026.

Chapter 04 / Development

A change in programme, a continuing inquiry

The transition from civic façades to housing retained an interest in occupied edges, climate and resident agency.

Responding to critique through a change in organisation

The record of the October feedback identifies a challenge to the project’s conceptual and architectural strength. My documented response was to select inhabited-threshold access as a direction for further development. Read retrospectively, the significant issue is whether this decision changes the organisation of the housing, rather than only its appearance. A stronger image would not, by itself, explain how residents enter, meet, withdraw or share space. [R113]

The inhabited route brings the project’s argument into circulation. It asks whether access can also provide a recognisable address and a place to pause. This creates a useful tension: the route must remain usable by someone who wants to pass through without participating. An inhabited gallery should not require residents to negotiate a social encounter every time they leave home. The proposal therefore has to accommodate both occupation and bypass. [R112–R113]

A counterfactual comparison would make this decision more rigorous. I propose to compare a conventional access arrangement with the inhabited-threshold arrangement while holding the site, building height and dwelling assumptions constant. I would compare route lengths, privacy at front doors, area allocated to movement and opportunities to pause. Any loss of efficiency needs to be made visible alongside the spatial benefit being claimed. This comparison has not been verified in the evidence reviewed for this draft, so I cannot yet present it as the reason one option performed better.

Draft basis: R113; feedback is user-reported, without a tutor transcript. R111–R114 establish the documented design sequence.

What the change of programme retained—and what it left behind

The journal should make the discontinuities in the project visible. The programme of a façade research centre did not survive the housing pivot unchanged. Neither should the earlier public audience or technical propositions be retrospectively presented as if they had always described housing. What remains relevant is an interest in environmental edges, occupation and the capacity of people to adjust their immediate surroundings. [R111]

The later work introduces questions that require different evidence: connections between households, the distinction between private and shared outdoor territory, and the relationship between massing and access. The sequence of references and adjacency studies records a developing direction, but it does not yet demonstrate that every option was evaluated. To make the Learning Journal credible, I need to retain rejected drawings and explain the trade-off that led to the next version, including decisions that remain unresolved. [R114, R116–R118]

Draft basis: R111; reconstructed chronology. R114 and R116–R118 record later design directions.

Chapter 05 / Proposal

The inhabited threshold

The proposal brings independent dwellings into proximity through a sequence of private, shared and public spaces.

The proposed housing arrangement

The dwelling system uses S, M, L and XL types to explore different household relationships. S is intended to sit beside M and above L, allowing proximity across generations while retaining the possibility of separate daily routines. XL explores co-residence within a larger home. These are organising rules rather than a final dwelling schedule; their value depends on the entrances, services, accessible routes and outdoor territories that accompany them. [R115–R116]

External access is intended to become an inhabited edge with recognisable front doors and space to pause. The gallery connects homes to the wider building, while the threshold mediates between movement and domestic territory. Planting and screening are proposed instruments within this sequence. Their location should follow the privacy, shelter and access questions established in the design method, with dimensions and sightlines demonstrated in the drawings. [R112]

The final presentation should make one complete sequence understandable: approach from the street, movement through the building, arrival at a household cluster and withdrawal into an independent home. Plans establish the relationships; the principal section connects them to levels, structure, outdoor space and occupation. The physical model should explain the same argument, including how homes can connect and separate. [R118]

Material decisions as part of the spatial argument

The material direction has developed from the earlier study of coloured metal and a brick base towards Paperbark cladding, brick at the lower levels, spotted gum and coloured circulation elements. I read this as an attempt to distinguish the building’s shared and domestic territories while responding to the surrounding brick context. The significance of the palette needs to be explained through where materials are encountered and used, rather than through colour associations alone.

A threshold brings these decisions together at a scale where they can be evaluated. The surface at the front door, the screen beside an occupied edge and the handrail along a shared route need to support the claimed experience of arrival. Their weathering, maintenance, fixing and relationship to drainage also matter. A thematic fragment should therefore connect the spatial argument to a plausible assembly, rather than operate as an isolated construction detail. [R117–R118]

The terraced massing raises a similar question. Outdoor territory depends on structure, waterproofing and support; it is not simply the space left around coloured dwelling blocks. The cantilever studies in the research record identify a technical question rather than a verified solution. I need to show where the proposed structure supports the argument and where it requires the geometry to change. Until this is coordinated, structural feasibility and environmental performance remain matters to establish, not benefits to claim. [R118]

Draft basis: R117–R118; later palette direction stated in the project conversation on 1 November 2026.

Chapter 06 / Reflection

What the project can demonstrate

Spatial proximity provides opportunities for contact. Its social outcomes also depend on residents, allocation and management.

What the proposal can responsibly claim

At this stage, the project offers a spatial proposition for balancing household independence with proximity. Its contribution is the attempt to make that balance explicit through entrances, connections and outdoor territories. The stronger claim—that the arrangement improves care, reduces isolation or produces a more supportive community—would require evidence beyond the drawings. Keeping these claims separate strengthens the argument by making its limits visible. [R115–R116]

The research also exposes an ethical risk in equating proximity with care. Family members may value living nearby without being able or willing to provide ongoing support. Shared facilities require cleaning, maintenance and decisions about access. An architectural proposal should not quietly assign that work to residents or imply that professional care can be replaced by a neighbouring relative. The design can accommodate contact and services; it cannot guarantee the relationships through which support occurs. [R105, R108]

A useful stress test is whether the housing remains credible when adjacent homes are occupied by unrelated households, or when a family’s circumstances change. Independent access and private retreat would then need to work without the optional family connection. Allocation and turnover also affect whether relatives can occupy adjacent homes in the first place. These operational questions should sit beside the spatial proposal rather than disappear from it. [R109–R110, R116]

Draft basis: R105, R108–R110, R115–R116; critical interpretation of the recorded research and design intentions.

Reflection on method and the next test

The distinction between an intention and a demonstrated result is the most useful organising principle for this exegesis. The research archive records a broad set of ideas, but its size does not establish the quality of the design decisions. The written argument needs to identify the smaller number of decisions that changed the project and show how they can be examined in drawings, models and comparisons.

For the next stage, I would prioritise one complete household cluster, one inhabited access sequence and one material threshold. Together these could connect the research question to occupancy, circulation and construction. Each should include a contrasting condition: a closed family link, a resident who does not want to pause, or an exposed edge requiring shelter. This would reveal whether the proposal remains useful when its preferred social scenario does not occur.

This reflection is written retrospectively on 3 November 2026. It interprets the available project record and identifies outstanding tests; it is not a substitute for the dated Learning Journal. The completed exegesis needs to pair this account with the original studies, actual feedback and subsequent decisions. Its conclusion should then describe what those tests established, what changed because of them and what remains beyond the scope of the project.

Draft basis: Assessment requirements: Learning Journal, revised methods, counterfactual options and material development. R111–R118 provide the project evidence trail.

Provisional conclusion

The project positions the domestic threshold as a place where multigenerational proximity can be negotiated. Its central proposition is that connection becomes more credible when each household also retains independence. The shift from the civic edge to the front door has therefore changed the programme while sharpening the question of who controls an encounter and who can withdraw from it.

The design argument is carried by the relationship between dwelling types, optional connections, inhabited access and private outdoor territory. The next step is to substantiate that relationship through the actual cluster plans, counterfactual access comparison and material fragment. The completed conclusion should identify the outcomes of those tests, including the compromises they reveal. Until then, the contribution is a clearly framed spatial proposition and a method for evaluating it, rather than a demonstrated solution to the social conditions of multigenerational living.
